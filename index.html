01 · The invitation

Start by listening. Demonstrate only what staff want to examine.

Civic Continuum is developing Maya Node around one principle: technical capability does not confer authority. We are asking whether a limited workflow demonstration could help make a handoff, exception, or follow-up easier to understand—not asking the food bank to replace its existing systems.

REQUEST → AUTHORITY → PERMISSION → EVIDENCE → HUMAN REVIEW → OUTCOME → CORRECTION

This is the accountability pattern we want to explore. People retain the authority to decide, approve, accept, and correct.

White Center Food Bank's staff know their operation best. We do not presume that current systems are deficient. Staff should identify whether a problem exists, which workflow is worth discussing, and what would count as useful.

02 · Respecting what's already there

Understanding the operating environment before suggesting change

Our project brief recognizes that White Center Food Bank coordinates food distribution, volunteer participation, home-delivery activity, and community partnerships. CERVIS and existing procedures are referenced as part of the environment. We have not conducted an internal audit and do not claim to know the complete inventory of software, tools, staffing, partner systems, or workflows.

What stays authoritative

  • Existing staff judgment and policies
  • CERVIS and current procedures
  • Partner relationships and established tools
  • WCFB's own decision-making authority

What we need to learn

  • Which systems are authoritative for each workflow
  • Where staff already record handoffs and exceptions
  • Where duplicate entry or ambiguity may occur, if anywhere
  • What accessibility, privacy, retention, and reporting rules apply
We are not claiming a confirmed gap. Questions about undocumented overrides, paper-only processes, system deficiencies, or partner integrations must be answered by WCFB—not assumed by us.
03 · Why consider us?

Potential value Civic Continuum and Maya Node could bring

The proposed value is a narrow accountability layer that may make ownership and next steps more visible alongside existing operations. These are benefits to test, not promised results.

Clearer accountability

Make it easier to see who owns a step and who is authorized to decide.

More legible handoffs

Keep the request, human decision, acknowledgement, and next step together in a structured view.

Exception visibility

Help distinguish an unusual case from routine work and identify the person who must review it.

Follow-through

Make assigned owners, open actions, and human-entered closeout notes easier to review.

Governance and learning

If WCFB chooses, support review of recurring themes and corrective actions without taking decisions away from staff.

Respect for existing tools

Explore a small complement to current systems rather than displacing or duplicating them.

04 · Three fictional scenarios

What the exploratory demonstration could look like

These are discussion exercises—not reports of actual WCFB incidents. All names, IDs, dates, quantities, and events in a demo will be synthetic.

Scenario 1

Volunteer handoff

Question: Would a clearer record of who prepared, approved, and acknowledged a handoff help staff resolve later questions?

  1. Create a fictional batch record.
  2. Identify the role authorized to approve release under WCFB policy.
  3. Show a simulated handoff acknowledgement.
  4. Have a supervisor review the example and record the next step.

Potential benefit: clearer ownership and easier reconstruction of what was acknowledged and what remains open.

Not included: live dispatch, route optimization, driver tracking, proof of delivery, or a production custody record.

Scenario 2

Grocery-rescue exception

Question: When an unusual delivery or quantity discrepancy occurs, what information helps the authorized person decide what happens next?

  1. Record a fictional discrepancy, such as expected versus observed case count.
  2. Refer it to the role WCFB policy designates.
  3. Let the authorized human decide what action is appropriate.
  4. Record the decision and who owns the next step.

Potential benefit: make the exception owner, decision point, and next action easier to identify.

Not included: temperature monitoring, inspection, food-safety determination, acceptance authorization, or compliance certification.

Scenario 3

Follow-through on a concern

Question: How does a concern move from intake to an assigned owner, an authorized response, and a documented closeout?

  1. Enter a fictional concern.
  2. Assign a responsible role or owner.
  3. Record the human review and authorized next step.
  4. Add a human-entered completion note and flag any remaining follow-up.

Potential benefit: distinguish assigned, in-progress, awaiting-decision, and closed items; make ownership and next steps visible.

Not included: a real complaint-management service, staff notifications, repair verification, or an automatic guarantee of resolution.

05 · Pilot initiative

A limited, no-cost exploratory pilot—only if WCFB wants it

The first phase is a conversation and agreed fictional demonstration, not deployment into food-bank operations.

Included in the proposed first phase

  • No-cost initial discussion
  • One workflow selected by WCFB staff
  • Agreed fictional demonstration using synthetic data
  • Review of usefulness, burden, and limitations
  • Written summary of findings, including a finding that no change is needed

Explicitly excluded

  • No live system or CERVIS connection
  • No client, donor, volunteer, staff, address, or other personal data
  • No access to internal accounts, networks, or databases
  • No installation or changes to current procedures
  • No outbound organizational messages or automated notifications
  • No live dispatch, routing, tracking, or delivery verification

Any later work involving real data, systems, staff operations, or service delivery would require a separate written scope, authorization, and appropriate privacy, security, legal, insurance, and operational review. WCFB can decline or stop at any time.

06 · How we judge usefulness

Success measures and honest evaluation

  • Can staff explain the demonstrated workflow and identify the human decision-maker?
  • Does the demonstration make the handoff, assigned owner, or next step easier to identify?
  • Does it avoid real personal data and access to WCFB systems?
  • Can staff identify at least one possible benefit and one burden or limitation?
  • Does it fit alongside current practice, or does it duplicate work?
  • What must never be automated?
A valid outcome may be “this does not help us.” The purpose is to learn from the food bank, not to force a product into its operation.

08 · Next step

One conversation. One staff-selected workflow.

If leadership is interested, the next step is to ask staff which of the three areas—if any—is worth discussing. Before a demonstration, agree on its purpose, participants, duration, data boundaries, success measures, and stop conditions.

There is no obligation to proceed. No system access, installation, or operational change is part of this first step.
